Welcome to the Liberatory Tech Project! The purpose of this project is to critically consider how we bring out liberatory systems and futures using critical and speculative perspectives. The LibTech project takes multiple approaches by way of Black Digital Archives and early Black press. Both aspects of the project utilize Wynter’s notion of the Human to understand the ways that Blackness and technology interact and overlap.

On the Black digital archives side of the project, we aim to protect our archives from generative artificial intelligence. Generative artificial intelligence is known to both hallucinate and perpetuate harmful stereotypes, which are harmful to communities that already face these problems in disproportionate amounts, such as the Black community (Jiang et al., 2023). Additionally, due to GenAI’s hallucinatory nature, this particular form of technology has an ability to present alternative truths. The presence of such alternative truths, as argued by Spennemann (2025), can be dangerous in spaces of preservation. Thus, GenAI presents problems that we need to face as a community. GenAI relies heavily on automated processes to collect data for training, some of which is done in the form of web scraping (Brach et al., 2025). Brach also describes multiple approaches for defending specifically against Large Language Model-based scrapers. In recent work, we utilize some of those same methods in a defensive strategy, with the goal being focused less on LLM-based scraping and instead on preventing content from the digital archive from being automatically included in preprocessing datasets at all. Preventing Black digital archives from being swept into these massive databases is a form of protection. In applying these methods to prevent automatic scraping and inclusion, we must also consider that the only goal is not to prevent machines from extracting from our archives, but there is the additional goal of keeping human navigation in tact even when the technological defenses are at play; machine inaccessibility needs to be coupled with human accessibility. Thus, one of the purposes of the LibTech project is to protect Black digital archives and support the Human (Wynter, 2015).  

On the early Black press side, we examine how computing systems can support the preservation and the study of historical Black newspapers without treating AI  technologies as neutral or authoritative. These newspapers document Black political thoughts, community life, resistance, and self-representation, yet their complex layouts and deteriorated print quality often make them difficult for conventional optical character recognition(OCR) systems to process accurately.

In order to address this problem, we are working on developing a human-in-the-loop document analysis pipeline for historical Black newspapers. The system is designed to process scanned newspaper pages whose column structures, mixed typography, illustrations, advertisements, and physical degradation. After AI proposes layout segmentation and  OCR, human annotators remain responsible for reviewing, correcting, and approving the final record. Throughout this process the platform preserves the annotation history, identifies disagreements between annotators, and supports consensus and administrative review. This approach helps prevent uncertain AI generated interpretations from silently becoming the ground truth for our dataset. 

Drawing from Wynter’s notion of the ‘Human’, this work asks who is recognized as a legitimate producer of knowledge and whose histories become legible within technical systems. And rather than using technology for the early Black press, we aim to build an infrastructure that supports careful human interpretations, accountability, and community stewardship. The resulting models, and materials will be exported in open archival formats to be used as part of Douglas' day collaborating with Zooniverse, helping make early Black newspapers more accessible while preserving provenance and human authority over their representation.
