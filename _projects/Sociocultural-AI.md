This project involves using individual-level (i.e., cognitive models) and social-level (i.e., sociocultural knowledge used by cognitive models) perspectives to study interactions between individuals and AI systems. We are using a cognitive architecture to model individual interactions with certain AI systems (agents) and particularly understand how and why racialization of AI agents may affect cooperation with those agents/systems.

We can separate our general work into three main subprojects: Exploring the racialization of AI systems, Understanding carceral algorithms and their impacts on the narratives of the incarcerated, Orienting 1st year engineering students for Critical AI literacy.

## Exploring the racialization of AI systems

### Pigchase Task

This study examined whether coordination with an AI agent is influenced by participants’ beliefs about the racial composition of the behavioral data used to train the agent. The current iteration is an extension to a previous study where Atkins et al. (2021) found that White participants scored higher with White-racialized or non-racialized AI agents and Black participants scored better with Black-racialized AI agents. We expanded on this study to investigate whether racial information-presented either explicitly or through visual priming-produced latent effects on human-AI cooperation. In our current online experiment, N= 1008 participants interacted with an AI agent under several experimental conditions. Participants from three self-identified races (Black, White, N-BW(Non-Black, Non-White)) interacted with AI from one of the seven experimental conditions, where they were told that data from people belonging to a particular race had informed the AI’s training. Retaining some conditions from original study, race was referenced only through textual descriptions for two conditions; a control condition included no racial information; four conditions presented pictures of individuals as visual racial primes (two for each race).

Participant performance was evaluated based on their task scores with higher scores indicating an effective coordination with the AI agent in catching the pig, and lower scores indicated reduced cooperation caused by either early task exit, or failure to catch the pig. Contrast to the results from Atkins et al. (2021), we observed that Black participants generally perceived AI as more intelligent and described the AI as cooperative and intelligent across conditions, even when their task scores were lower. White participants on the other hand reported mixed perceptions of Black-racialized agents but evaluated agents in the Black-racialized and control conditions as less intelligent, with particularly negative responses toward the AI in control condition. Participants categorized as N-BW (Non-Black or Non-White) perceived the least cooperation and the greatest opposition in the Black-racialized conditions while responding more favorably to White-racialized agents. Overall, the White-racialized conditions received the highest positive and lowest negative evaluations across demographic groups. The results are reported in Dulam and Dancy 2025.

We used ACT-R cognitive architecture to simulate participant behavior and analyze the cognitive mechanisms underlying interaction with the AI agent. We developed several ACT-R models like rational model, instance based model, trust accumulator based model to fit participants' strategies. The rational model provided a good fit for participants in the White and N-BW groups but did not fit the behavior of Black participants as closely. Although the trust accumulator based model produced a comparatively better fit for Black participants, its fit remained weaker than the fits obtained for the other participant groups. Therefore, this work is still ongoing.
